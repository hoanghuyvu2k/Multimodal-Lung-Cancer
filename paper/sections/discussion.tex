\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We extended the DyAM multimodal attention framework with two contributions, a competitive (OvO) attention mechanism with fewer
parameters and an NLP-based encoding of tabular clinical features, and evaluated them on response (AUC) and progression-free survival in a
247-patient cohort, with 5~seeds~$\times$~10-fold repeated cross-validation over 21 modality combinations.
The experiments support four conclusions.

\paragraph{Fusing sources is what moves performance.}
The best single source was PD-L1~TPS ($0.720 \pm 0.012$), followed by genomics ($0.676$), CT radiomics ($0.672$) and TMB ($0.616$).
Fusing four sources raised the mean AUC to $0.763 \pm 0.022$ and adding the NLP-encoded clinical variables to $0.786 \pm 0.017$,
$0.066$ above PD-L1~TPS, the biomarker in routine use, and above the modest discrimination reported for PD-L1 and TMB
\cite{Lu2019,Rizvi2015}. The ensemble of the five partitions reached $0.799$, close to the $0.80$ reported for the original framework
\cite{Vanguri2022}.
The same pattern held for survival: the association of the risk score with progression-free survival rose from a C-index of
$0.567$--$0.587$ for a single source to $0.588$--$0.627$ for five to seven sources, and the 12-month time-dependent AUC from
$0.596$--$0.661$ to $0.657$--$0.709$. In a leave-one-source-out analysis of OvO+NLP at the primary benchmark (Supplementary Note~S4),
removing any single source lowered the mean AUC in 4 or 5 of 5 seeds, by $0.013$ (NLP-encoded clinical variables) to $0.029$ (pathology),
so every source contributed. Two endpoints and a leave-one-out analysis therefore point to the same conclusion, that the sources carry
complementary information that attention-based fusion can combine.

\paragraph{The NLP encoding of clinical variables is better than numeric labs.}
This was the most consistent finding of the study. Replacing the 13 numeric laboratory and clinical values by their sentence embedding
raised the mean AUC in all six comparisons that were made: $+0.017$ and $+0.020$ in the two pathology arms under cooperative attention,
$+0.014$ and $+0.024$ in the two seven-source configurations under cooperative attention, and $+0.022$ and $+0.019$ in the same two
configurations under OvO attention, so the benefit appeared with both attention mechanisms and both pathology encodings.
A plausible reason is that a sentence embedding represents combinations of variables, for example advanced age together with low albumin and
poor performance status, that are not explicit in a 13-dimensional numeric vector and that the attention module would otherwise have to learn from a
small training set \cite{Reimers2019,Wang2020MiniLM}; our data do not test this mechanism.
The AUC with the encoding was also above that of the same sources without any clinical variable ($+0.023$ for DyAM and $+0.014$ for OvO at the
primary benchmark), numeric labs were below that baseline in the arm analysis, and the general-purpose sentence encoder was better than the
domain-specific BioClinBERT in all five partitions (Supplementary Note~S2), so no clinical fine-tuning was needed.
For survival, the NLP encodings gave higher hazard ratios than no clinical encoding in both arms ($4.12$ to $4.43$--$4.52$ in IHC-A and $4.02$ to
$4.47$--$4.63$ in IHC-G), and at the primary benchmark the log hazard ratio rose by $+0.043$ when the NLP encoding alone was added to cooperative attention.

\paragraph{OvO attention is the better fusion mechanism for many sources, with a quarter of the parameters.}
OvO had the higher mean AUC at the primary benchmark ($0.771$ against $0.763$; four of five seeds) and by $+0.019$ to $+0.021$ at
Rad+IHC-A+MutAmp, Rad+IHC-A+Gen+PDL1 and Rad+IHC-A+Gen+TMB+PDL1, and by $+0.045$ at TMB+PDL1, each in all five seeds.
With the NLP encoding added as a source, OvO+NLP was above DyAM+NLP in all five seeds at four of the six configurations with five or more sources
($+0.010$ to $+0.028$), while using $1{,}242$ trainable parameters against $4{,}969$.
A learning-curve analysis is compatible with a parameter-economy explanation: with about $89$ training patients OvO+NLP reached $0.707$ and DyAM+NLP $0.677$,
and the gap closed as the training set grew to $222$ patients ($0.785$ and $0.786$), with neither curve levelled off, so the smaller model helps most where
data are scarcest, which is the situation of most multimodal clinical cohorts; we did not test the explanation by shrinking the cooperative attention matrix.
The survival results favoured OvO more clearly than the AUC. In each of the eight configurations with five to seven sources, the mean log hazard ratio, the
12-month time-dependent AUC and the C-index of OvO were above those of cooperative attention. At the primary benchmark OvO with the NLP encoding had a higher
log hazard ratio than DyAM in all five seeds ($+0.070$, patient-bootstrap interval $0.013$ to $0.106$, which excludes zero) and a higher C-index in all five seeds,
and the ordering was reproduced in two other software environments ($+0.070$ and $+0.076$). OvO with the NLP encoding also gave the highest log hazard ratio
($0.495$ and $0.497$ against $0.416$ and $0.411$ for DyAM, higher in all five seeds) and 12-month time-dependent AUC ($0.708$ and $0.704$ against $0.675$ and $0.678$) in both
configurations that contain labs. The cooperative model remained stronger in a few small configurations, such as IHC-G+Gen
($-0.028$ AUC) and radiomics-only, so the choice of mechanism may depend on the number and kind of sources; the two formulations coincide for a single source.

\paragraph{The approach is practical.}
Encoding a patient profile takes less than one second on CPU, needs no labelled clinical text, and can be applied to any structured electronic health record
field that can be written as a sentence, so it can be transferred to other structured clinical datasets without a fine-tuned clinical language model.
An availability vector, which tells the model which sources a patient has, raised the AUC by $0.015$--$0.017$ in the two Gen configurations without PD-L1 but only
by $-0.003$ to $+0.008$ in the others (Supplementary Note~S5), and was not part of the main model.

\paragraph{Performance estimates.}
A harder evaluation gave lower values: training on the patients with the earliest CT accession numbers and testing on the later 124 (a proxy for time, since calendar
dates were not available) gave AUCs of $0.703$ ($0.587$--$0.819$) for OvO+NLP and $0.700$ ($0.587$--$0.812$) for DyAM+NLP, with $0.707$ and $0.678$ at seven sources.
The cross-validated AUCs are therefore best read as the performance under the repeated cross-validation protocol rather than the expected performance in new
patients. Because about 60 variants were compared on the same 247 patients, the highest figures also include some selection, which we estimate at $0.014$--$0.031$ AUC.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
First, this was a single-institution retrospective cohort, which may limit generalisability and introduce selection bias.
Second, with $n = 247$ (62 responders) the 95\% interval of an AUC is about $\pm 0.07$, and the differences between models, which are $0.02$ AUC or smaller
at the primary benchmark, are smaller than this cohort can resolve (about 1{,}200--1{,}350 patients would be needed to detect $0.02$ AUC with 80\% power;
Supplementary Note~S3), so they are described by direction and magnitude.
Third, results depended on the numerical environment (in a reproduction check the same code gave $0.798$ on Linux and $0.788$ on Windows), so all results
are from one pinned environment, and the repeated-seed analyses use the same patients in every partition, so they quantify partition variability and not variability across patients.
Fourth, the sentence encoder was used without fine-tuning on NSCLC clinical text.
Fifth, calendar dates were not available, so temporal validation was approximated by CT accession order and covered two configurations.
Finally, PD-L1 TPS was scored with the Sauter method, which may differ from the SP142 or 22C3 assays used elsewhere, so PD-L1-derived features should be
interpreted with this in mind.
